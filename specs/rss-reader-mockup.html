Germany's population is projected to shrink by nearly 5 per cent within 25 years — a significantly steeper decline than previously forecast, according to an Ifo study.

The German economic think-tank on Tuesday revised its forecast for a 1 per cent population decline by 2050 to nearly 5 per cent — a drop that would leave Germany with its smallest population since 1990. The revision is based on updated figures from the country's statistical office.

"Demographic change will have significant effects on all areas of the economy and society," Ifo economist Joachim Ragnitz warned in the study.

✦ TL;DR

A growing body of research contradicts the popular narrative that smartphones are harming teen mental health. Multiple large-scale studies find minimal or no causal effects, though the debate remains politically charged.

Multiple studies have either shown that smartphone and social media use among teens has minimal effects on their mental health, or have been unable to establish a clear causal link between the two.

The research landscape

A comprehensive meta-analysis published last year found that the effect size of social media on teen well-being was "trivially small" — comparable to the effect of wearing glasses on academic performance. The findings challenge the narrative popularized by Jonathan Haidt's "The Anxious Generation" and others.

Meanwhile, a new longitudinal study from Oxford tracked over 12,000 adolescents across six years and found that social media use at one point in time did not predict mental health outcomes at later points, once demographic and personality factors were controlled for.

"The evidence for a causal link remains remarkably thin given the strength of public conviction on this topic," noted Dr. Andrew Przybylski, one of the study's lead authors.

This doesn't mean screens are good for teens — it means the relationship is far more nuanced than the "phones are destroying a generation" framing suggests. Displacement effects (less sleep, less exercise) may matter more than the content itself.

Senator Bill Cassidy (R-La.) released a new report on how to modernize the FDA. It has some good material.

FDA's processes were designed for an era of simple chemical drugs, not the complex biologics, gene therapies, and AI-driven diagnostics that now dominate the pipeline. The report proposes restructuring the agency around therapeutic areas rather than product types — a shift that would mirror how the EMA already operates.

The most interesting proposal: creating a "conditional approval" pathway that would allow promising treatments to reach patients faster while requiring ongoing real-world evidence collection. This borrows from the approach already used for accelerated approval in oncology.
